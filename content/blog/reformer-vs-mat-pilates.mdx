**The short answer:** mat Pilates uses only your body weight and gravity; reformer Pilates adds a spring-loaded carriage that can both assist and resist your movement. Neither is "better" — the reformer gives beginners more support and advanced students more challenge, while mat work builds unassisted core control you can practice anywhere. Most people get the best results from a practice that includes both.

## What's actually different

**Mat Pilates** is the original format Joseph Pilates designed: a sequence of exercises performed on a padded mat, using your own body weight as resistance. Props like rings, bands, and small balls sometimes join in, but the work is fundamentally you versus gravity.

**Reformer Pilates** happens on a machine — a sliding carriage attached to a frame with adjustable springs, a footbar, and straps. The springs create resistance you push and pull against, and, counterintuitively, they can also *assist* you, supporting part of your body weight while you learn a movement pattern.

## The case for the reformer

- **It meets you where you are.** Spring tension is adjustable per exercise, so the same movement can be gentler than mat work for a beginner or dramatically harder for an athlete.
- **Feedback you can feel.** The moving carriage tells you immediately when you're rushing, gripping, or working one side harder — feedback a mat can't give.
- **Support for injuries and limitations.** Because springs can assist, reformer work is often the entry point for people managing back pain, joint issues, or post-rehab conditioning (with your doctor's clearance).
- **Resistance training without impact.** You build genuine strength — legs, glutes, back, arms — with zero jumping and minimal joint load.

## The case for the mat

- **Nothing between you and the work.** Without springs to help, your core stabilizes everything. Mat work exposes — and fixes — the weaknesses machines can mask.
- **It travels.** Hotel room, living room, park: your practice goes where you go.
- **Lower cost.** Mat classes are usually a bit cheaper than reformer classes, and practicing at home costs nothing.

## Which should a beginner start with?

We usually point complete beginners to one of two doors:

1. **A beginner reformer class** — the springs support you while you learn the vocabulary, and the equipment keeps you honest about alignment. Our beginner classes are slow-paced and built around the fundamentals.
2. **A private session or two** — one-on-one time on the reformer gets you comfortable with the equipment before you join a group.

If you're nervous about machines, start on the mat — every principle transfers. But don't let the reformer intimidate you; it was literally designed to *help*.

## Which burns more / builds more?

Both formats build core strength, flexibility, and posture. The reformer's added resistance generally builds more visible full-body strength, especially in legs and glutes, while mat work develops exceptional trunk control. For most goals — feeling stronger, standing taller, easing a stiff back, toning up — consistency matters far more than format. Two to three sessions a week of either will outperform occasional sessions of the "perfect" one.
