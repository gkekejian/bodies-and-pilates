## The machine, minus the intimidation

A reformer looks like a narrow bed frame with a sliding platform, a set of springs, some straps, and a bar at one end. First-timers often stare at it the way they would stare at gym equipment with no instructions. Here is the reassuring part: you do not need to understand the machine before your first class. Your instructor sets it up, tells you where to put your hands and feet, and adjusts everything for you. By the end of class, the parts that looked mysterious will feel obvious.

The parts that matter: the carriage (the sliding platform you sit, lie, kneel, or stand on), the springs (color-coded by resistance, attached between the carriage and the frame), the footbar (for pushing with your feet or hands), and the straps (for arm and leg work). That is the whole machine.

## The springs: your resistance dial

The springs are the reason the reformer works so well for beginners and advanced clients alike. Heavier springs mean more resistance for strength work. Lighter springs mean less assistance, which sounds backwards until you try it: with light springs, your stabilizing muscles have to do all the work, and a simple movement becomes surprisingly demanding.

Your instructor chooses your spring settings for every exercise. You will learn the colors quickly, but you never need to memorize anything. If a setting feels wrong, say so. Adjusting springs mid-class is normal.

## What a 50-minute class feels like

A typical class opens with a warm-up series on the carriage: footwork, gentle articulation, breathing. The middle of class moves through full-body sequences. Expect leg work on the footbar, arm work with the straps, core series that will humble you, and balance challenges that remind you how much your small muscles do. It closes with stretching on the reformer, which feels earned.

The defining sensation is controlled shaking. Not pain, not exhaustion, but your muscles working in a deep, precise way that is hard to replicate with weights. You will be warm but rarely drenched. You will feel taller walking out. The next day, you will discover muscles you forgot you owned. That is normal, and it fades as your body adapts.

## Your first class, step by step

Arrive 10 minutes early. You will meet your instructor, get a quick tour, and get set up on your reformer before anyone else arrives. Wear form-fitting clothes and [grip socks](/faq#wear) (required, and sold at the front desk if you do not have a pair). Tell your instructor about any injuries or conditions before class starts. This is not optional politeness; it changes which exercises they give you.

During class, watch the instructor's demonstrations, listen for the spring changes, and do not worry about keeping up perfectly. Everyone in the room had a first class. After class, your instructor will usually check in. Ask questions. That conversation is part of what you paid for.

## Common first-timer worries

"I am not flexible enough." Flexibility is a result of Pilates, not a prerequisite. Start where you are.

"I have never done anything like this." Most of our new clients have not. The [Beginner class](/classes/beginner) exists specifically for you.

"Will I be the only new person?" Sometimes, and it does not matter. Small classes mean the instructor adapts to the room. Being new in a small class gets you more attention, not less.

"What if I cannot finish an exercise?" Then you rest, and rejoin when you are ready. Modifying is not failing. It is training.

The reformer stops being intimidating about fifteen minutes into your first class. What replaces the intimidation, for most people, is curiosity about what their body can learn to do.
