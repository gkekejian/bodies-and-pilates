"I would love to try Pilates, but I am not flexible enough."

Instructors hear some version of this every week. It is understandable, and it is also backwards. Saying you are too stiff for Pilates is like saying you are too out of shape to go to the gym. Flexibility is not the entry ticket. It is one of the things you build once you start.

## Flexibility is the outcome, not the prerequisite

Pilates was designed as a system for building strength and mobility together. The whole point is controlled movement through a full range of motion, repeated consistently. Nobody walks into their first class touching their toes with straight legs. Most people cannot, and the class is built for exactly those people.

If you can sit, stand, and lie down, you can start. Everything else is adjustable, which brings us to the machine itself.

## How the reformer meets you where you are

The reformer looks intimidating the first time you see one. All those springs and straps. In practice, it is one of the most beginner-friendly pieces of fitness equipment you will find, because it adapts to your body instead of asking your body to adapt to it.

The springs provide assistance as well as resistance. A tight hamstring that would make a standing stretch miserable becomes manageable when a strap and a light spring are guiding the movement. The carriage supports your body weight, so your joints are not loaded the way they are in standing exercise. Footbar positions, spring loads, and strap lengths all adjust in seconds, which means your instructor can scale every exercise to your current range of motion without singling you out.

This is why stiff beginners often progress faster on the reformer than they do in a mat or yoga class. The machine does part of the work of supporting you, so you can focus on the movement itself.

## What actually happens in a Beginner class

At Bodies and Pilates, our [Beginner classes](/classes/beginner) in North Hollywood are paced for people who are new to the method. Your instructor demonstrates each exercise, then coaches the room through it with clear form cues. Because class sizes stay small, you get individual corrections, including the ones that matter most when you are stiff: where to place your feet, how to position your pelvis, when to back off a range that is not yours yet.

Nobody is expected to match the person on the next reformer. The person next to you might be on their fiftieth class. You are on your first. The only comparison that matters is you last week versus you this week, and that comparison starts moving in the right direction fast.

## How quickly will you feel a difference?

Most new clients notice changes in how they move within three to four weeks of coming twice a week. Reaching for something on a high shelf gets easier. Sitting at a desk all day hurts less. That is mobility improving, and it compounds. Dramatic flexibility, the kind you see on Instagram, takes months of consistent practice, and it is not required to get the benefits. You do not need to become a contortionist. You just need to move a little better than last month, every month.

## The only real prerequisites

You do not need flexibility. You do not need experience, coordination, or a dance background. What helps: form-fitting clothes you can move in, grip socks (required, and we sell them at the front desk), and arriving ten minutes early so your instructor can show you the reformer before class starts.

That is the whole list. If you have an injury or a medical condition, mention it when you book so we can guide you to the right class or suggest a [private session](/classes/private) first.

Being stiff is not a reason to wait. It is the reason to start.
