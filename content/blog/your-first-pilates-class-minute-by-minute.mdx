The hardest part of your first Pilates class is walking through the door. Everything after that is choreography, and it helps to know the steps in advance. Here is exactly what happens at Bodies and Pilates in North Hollywood, minute by minute.

## Before you arrive

Book the [Beginner class](/classes/beginner), not the Full Body. Wear form-fitting workout clothes, nothing baggy. [Grip socks are required](/faq#wear) on the reformer, and we sell them at the front desk if you do not own a pair. Eat something light an hour or two before, and bring water.

[Parking](/faq#parking): street parking on Weddington St and metered spots in front of the studio on Vineland Ave. Give yourself a few extra minutes to park and find us at 5251 Vineland Ave, Suite 6.

## Minutes -10 to 0: arrival and setup

Arrive ten minutes early. This is the most important instruction in this post, because everything pleasant about your first class depends on not rushing. At the front desk you will check in, meet whoever is working, and get a quick tour of the studio.

Then your instructor introduces themselves and walks you to your reformer. They will set your springs, show you the footbar, the straps, and the carriage, and explain the handful of terms you will hear: neutral spine, powerhouse, carriage, springs. Nobody expects you to remember all of it. The instructor will repeat every setup cue during class.

## Minutes 0 to 10: warm-up

Class starts with everyone on their reformers. The warm-up is gentle by design: breathing, footwork, basic articulation. The instructor demonstrates each exercise, then walks the room correcting form. If you are doing something wrong, they will fix it kindly and specifically. That is the job, and in a small class it happens constantly. Do not be embarrassed; being corrected means you are being coached.

## Minutes 10 to 40: the work

This is the main block, and it moves through the body in sections: legs, core, arms, more core. Expect exercises with names that mean nothing to you yet, like teaser prep, side-lying series, and long stretch. Each one gets a setup explanation, a demonstration, and then you do it while the instructor circulates.

Two things surprise every beginner. First, how hard small movements are: an inch of controlled motion on heavy springs will humble you. Second, how fast fifty minutes goes when you are concentrating. Around minute thirty you will probably think some version of "I cannot do another rep," and then you will do five more, because the instructor knows exactly where your limit actually is.

## Minutes 40 to 50: cool down and stretch

The last ten minutes bring the intensity down: stretching on and off the reformer, breathing, the kind of deep hip and hamstring work that makes you understand why people describe Pilates as addictive. You will leave the studio feeling taller than when you arrived. That part is not marketing; decompression after spinal articulation genuinely changes how you stand.

## After class

Grab your things, and expect the instructor to check in with you. Tell them how it felt; the feedback helps them program your next class. You will probably be sore tomorrow in your abs, glutes, and inner thighs. That is normal adaptation, not injury, and it fades as your body learns the work.

Book your second class before you leave, or at least within the week. The first class teaches you the vocabulary. The second class is when it starts feeling like exercise instead of orientation.

## The things everyone worries about

"I am not flexible enough." Flexibility is the outcome, not the prerequisite. "Everyone will be better than me." Beginner classes exist precisely so that is not true. "I will not know what I am doing." Correct, and that is why the instructor is there. "What if I need to stop?" Then you stop, rest, and rejoin. Nobody minds, and nobody is watching.

The door is the hard part. Everything after it is taken care of.
